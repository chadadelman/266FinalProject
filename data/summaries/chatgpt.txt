A_Midsummer_Nights_Dream-Act I-Scene I
Theseus and Hippolyta plan their upcoming wedding. Egeus arrives with his daughter Hermia, demanding she marry Demetrius instead of Lysander, whom she loves. Theseus upholds Athenian law: Hermia must obey her father or face death or lifelong chastity. After the court leaves, Hermia and Lysander plan to flee into the forest to marry. Helena, secretly in love with Demetrius, learns of their plan and decides to tell Demetrius in hopes of winning his affection.

A_Midsummer_Nights_Dream-Act I-Scene II
A group of Athenian tradesmen (“the Mechanicals”) meet to assign roles for their play, which they hope to perform at Theseus’ wedding. Peter Quince directs. Nick Bottom enthusiastically wants every role, showing comic overconfidence. They agree to rehearse in the forest so they can practice in private.

A_Midsummer_Nights_Dream-Act II-Scene I
In the forest, the fairy world is introduced. Oberon and Titania, king and queen of the fairies, argue over a changeling boy whom Titania refuses to give up. Their conflict has disrupted natural order. Oberon plans revenge using a magical flower that makes people fall in love with the first creature they see. Meanwhile, Puck observes Demetrius angrily rejecting Helena, who follows him hopelessly. Oberon pities her and orders Puck to use the magic on Demetrius.

A_Midsummer_Nights_Dream-Act II-Scene II
Titania falls asleep, and Oberon applies the love-charm to her eyes. Elsewhere, Hermia and Lysander also sleep in the forest. Puck finds Lysander and Hermia and mistakes Lysander for Demetrius. He enchants Lysander, who wakes to see Helena and immediately becomes infatuated with her. Helena, thinking he mocks her, is confused and distressed. Hermia awakens alone and terrified.

A_Midsummer_Nights_Dream-Act III-Scene I
The Mechanicals rehearse in the forest. Puck mischievously transforms Bottom’s head into that of a donkey. The others flee in fear, but Bottom sings to show he’s not afraid. Titania wakes, sees transformed Bottom, and instantly falls in love due to the enchantment. She commands her fairies to serve and pamper him.

A_Midsummer_Nights_Dream-Act III-Scene II
Oberon discovers Puck enchanted the wrong man. He enchants Demetrius properly, causing Demetrius to awaken in love with Helena as well. Now both Lysander and Demetrius pursue Helena. Helena believes they are cruelly mocking her. Hermia arrives and becomes furious that Lysander now rejects her. The four lovers argue bitterly. To stop the escalating chaos, Puck separates them in the forest and magically exhausts them into sleep. He restores Lysander’s feelings to Hermia but leaves Demetrius enchanted.

A_Midsummer_Nights_Dream-Act IV-Scene I
Titania dotes comically on Bottom, who enjoys the attention. Oberon, now satisfied, removes the enchantment from her. She awakens horrified by the donkey-headed lover she adored. Together, they reconcile. Puck restores Bottom to human form. Theseus, Hippolyta, and Egeus stumble upon the sleeping young lovers. Demetrius announces he now loves Helena, resolving the conflict. Theseus overrules Egeus and decides all three couples will marry together.

A_Midsummer_Nights_Dream-Act IV-Scene II
Back in Athens, the Mechanicals believe Bottom has disappeared and fear their play will be ruined. Just as they plan to give up, Bottom returns, telling them the show must go on. They prepare excitedly for the performance.

A_Midsummer_Nights_Dream-Act V-Scene I
The three couples celebrate their weddings. The Mechanicals perform “Pyramus and Thisbe,” a hilariously clumsy and over-acted play that entertains the court. After the humans retire for the night, Oberon, Titania, and the fairies bless the marriages and the house. Puck closes the play with a final speech asking the audience for forgiveness if the performance seemed like a dream.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act I-Scene I
In Rousillon, the Countess mourns her late husband as her son Bertram prepares to leave for the French court. Helena, the orphaned daughter of a renowned physician, reveals her secret love for Bertram. Parolles banters with Helena about virginity, exposing his shallow worldview. After Bertram departs, Helena resolves to follow him to Paris and attempt to cure the King using her father’s medical knowledge.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act I-Scene II
At court, the King of France reflects on his illness and the memory of Bertram’s late father, whom he admired deeply. Despite his noble responsibilities, he refuses treatment, believing no physician can help him. His lords advise him on political matters and a gathering of young noblemen prepares for military service. Bertram’s arrival is anticipated warmly.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act I-Scene III
The Countess learns that Helena is in love with Bertram and gently urges her to be honest. Helena confesses her feelings and her plan to cure the King in Paris. The Countess blesses her endeavor and promises unwavering support. Helena departs for court with renewed resolve.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act II-Scene I
At the French court, Helena pleads with the King to allow her to attempt a cure, offering her life as the forfeit if she fails. The King reluctantly agrees, impressed by her confidence and sincerity. She administers the treatment, and a hopeful anticipation spreads through the court. Helena prepares for the outcome with courage.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act II-Scene II
Back in Rousillon, the Countess scolds her clown, Lavache, about his behavior in a comedic exchange. A letter arrives from Helena explaining her mission to heal the King. The Countess publicly praises Helena’s bravery and virtue. She wishes deeply for Helena’s success.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act II-Scene III
The King, now cured, gratefully fulfills his promise to Helena by allowing her to choose any nobleman as her husband. Helena chooses Bertram, who reacts with refusal and disdain. The King commands him to obey, praising Helena’s virtue and accomplishments. Bertram reluctantly submits but inwardly rejects the marriage.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act II-Scene IV
Bertram and Parolles privately discuss plans to escape the forced marriage. Bertram asks Parolles to accompany him to the Tuscan wars. Parolles eagerly accepts the task, feeding Bertram’s frustration with Helena. They begin arranging their departure from court.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act II-Scene V
Lafeu and Parolles argue after Lafeu mocks Parolles’ pretensions. As Bertram prepares to leave, he hands Helena a cold farewell. Despite her attempts at reconciliation, Bertram remains distant and determined to flee. Helena senses his resentment but remains committed to him.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act III-Scene I
At the camp of the Duke of Florence, the Duke welcomes the newly arrived French gentlemen. They discuss the politics of the war and the honorable role they will play. Bertram and his companions prepare to fight, eager for distinction. The Duke expresses gratitude for their support.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act III-Scene II
The Countess receives a letter from Bertram expressing his refusal to consummate the marriage. Helena, heartbroken, decides to leave Rousillon, believing her absence may cause Bertram to return. She departs secretly on a pilgrimage to Saint Jacques. The Countess mourns Helena’s sacrifice and prays for her safety.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act III-Scene III
Bertram, now serving in Florence, celebrates his military successes with Parolles and other gentlemen. Parolles proposes recovering a lost drum—an action the others suspect he is too cowardly to attempt. The lords decide to expose Parolles’ bravado. Bertram remains unaware of Parolles’ true nature.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act III-Scene IV
In Florence, the widow and her daughter Diana meet Helena, who is disguised as a pilgrim. Helena learns that Bertram has been aggressively pursuing Diana. The widow and Diana sympathize with Helena’s suffering. A bond begins to form between them.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act III-Scene V
A group of Florentine women discuss the war and Bertram’s flirtations. They warn Diana about the dangers of yielding to his advances. Helena listens as the women praise Diana’s chastity and caution her about Bertram’s intentions. Their conversation provides Helena with clarity for her plan.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act III-Scene VI
Bertram’s fellow soldiers discuss how to expose Parolles’ cowardice. They plan an elaborate ruse in which Parolles will be captured by supposed enemies. The scheme promises both entertainment and justice. Bertram agrees to watch the spectacle unfold.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act III-Scene VII
Helena reveals her plan to the widow and Diana: Diana will agree to meet Bertram, but Helena will secretly take her place in the dark. This will allow Helena to gain Bertram’s ring and his child, fulfilling the conditions he set. The widow and Diana agree to help. They begin preparing the details of the deception.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act IV-Scene I
Parolles goes to retrieve the lost drum and is ambushed by Bertram’s fellow soldiers disguised as enemy captors. Blindfolded and terrified, he immediately betrays his comrades and reveals military secrets. His cowardice becomes undeniable. The soldiers prepare to mock him further.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act IV-Scene II
Diana meets with Bertram, who continues to pressure her. Under Helena’s guidance, Diana sets the stage for the bed-trick by agreeing to a nighttime meeting. She demands that Bertram give her his ring, which he does impulsively. Bertram is unaware that Helena will take Diana’s place.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act IV-Scene III
Parolles, still blindfolded, is interrogated and confesses to insulting Bertram and lying about his own valor. When the blindfold is removed, he realizes he has been tricked by his own companions. Bertram, embarrassed, rejects Parolles entirely. Orders arrive for Bertram to return to France, and he imagines Helena dead.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act IV-Scene IV
Helena writes letters to the King and to the widow explaining that she is alive and carrying out her plan. She resolves to travel to Saint Jacques. Her departure ensures Bertram will believe she is dead until she reveals the truth at the right moment. She leaves Florence quietly.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act IV-Scene V
In Rousillon, the Countess speaks with Lafeu about Bertram’s behavior and Helena’s supposed death. Lafeu expresses disappointment in Bertram but offers to arrange a marriage between Bertram and his own daughter. The Countess hopes Helena might somehow still be alive. Their conversation sets the stage for Bertram’s return.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act V-Scene I
Helena arrives near the French court and learns that the King is hosting Bertram and Lafeu. She prepares to reveal the truth and deliver Diana’s petition. Still disguised as a pilgrim, she approaches cautiously. Her appearance promises resolution to the unfolding confusion.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act V-Scene II
Back in Rousillon, Parolles returns in disgrace and is mocked by Lavache. Surprisingly, Parolles accepts his fall with humility and requests simple employment. The Countess and others pity him. His storyline closes on a quieter, more reflective note.

Alls_Well_That_Ends_Well-Act V-Scene III
At court, the King confronts Bertram about the ring he gave away and his inconsistent behavior. Diana enters and accuses Bertram of seducing her, further complicating matters. Just as confusion peaks, Helena appears alive, bearing Bertram’s ring and proof of her fulfillment of his conditions. Bertram, shocked but relieved, accepts her fully, and the King declares all wrongs resolved.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act I-Scene I
Philo and Demetrius criticize Antony for abandoning his Roman duties in favor of Cleopatra and the pleasures of Egypt. Antony and Cleopatra enter, displaying their passionate and theatrical relationship. Cleopatra challenges Antony to declare how much he loves her, prompting playful yet intense banter. A messenger arrives with news from Rome, but Antony refuses to hear it, insisting that nothing matters except their love.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act I-Scene II
Cleopatra interrogates her attendants about her health and omens. A messenger brings news that Fulvia, Antony’s wife, has revolted against Caesar and then died. Antony is shaken and realizes he must return to Rome. Cleopatra accuses him of insincerity, and Antony struggles between desire and duty, ultimately deciding to leave Egypt.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act I-Scene III
Cleopatra angrily confronts Antony for his decision to return to Rome. She accuses him of abandoning her, using emotional manipulation to test his loyalty. Antony reassures her that his departure is political, not romantic. Despite her theatrics, Cleopatra ultimately reveals how deeply she fears losing him.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act I-Scene IV
In Rome, Caesar complains about Antony’s neglect of his responsibilities. He recalls Antony’s military greatness and laments how far he has fallen since living in Egypt. Lepidus attempts to defend Antony, but Caesar remains frustrated with the state of affairs. They prepare to address the growing unrest in the empire.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act I-Scene V
Cleopatra remains in Alexandria, longing for Antony. She reminisces about their past adventures and demands reports of his whereabouts. Her attendants attempt to soothe her, and a messenger brings news of Antony’s movements. Cleopatra continues to oscillate between tenderness and volatility, showing her emotional complexity.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act II-Scene I
Pompey and his allies discuss their growing strength against the Roman triumvirs. They note that Caesar and Lepidus are weakened by internal issues, while Antony is distracted in Egypt. Pompey believes the time is favorable for rebellion. His followers encourage him to seize the opportunity.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act II-Scene II
Lepidus calls a meeting with Caesar and Antony to resolve their disputes. Caesar accuses Antony of neglect and political irresponsibility. Agrippa proposes a marriage between Antony and Caesar’s sister Octavia to cement peace. Antony agrees, though Enobarbus privately notes that Antony will never leave Cleopatra behind.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act II-Scene III
A soothsayer warns Antony that Caesar’s fortune will overshadow his own and advises him to return to Egypt. Antony is troubled by the prophecy. He acknowledges Caesar’s growing power but accepts the political need to proceed with the planned marriage. The conflict between his destiny and desires deepens.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act II-Scene IV
Lepidus prepares for the military campaign and assigns commanders to various regions. He discusses the upcoming actions against Pompey. Messengers confirm the readiness of troops. Lepidus struggles to maintain unity among the triumvirs.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act II-Scene V
In Alexandria, Cleopatra awaits Antony impatiently. A messenger arrives with news of Antony’s marriage to Octavia. Cleopatra reacts violently, beating the messenger and threatening him. After calming down, she demands more details, insisting on knowing Octavia’s appearance. Her jealousy exposes her vulnerability.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act II-Scene VI
Pompey meets with the triumvirs and negotiates peace. Despite mutual distrust, they reach an agreement that grants Pompey control over Sicily and Sardinia. Enobarbus and Menas discuss the political situation, observing Antony’s character and Cleopatra’s influence. Tension lingers beneath the diplomatic façade.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act II-Scene VII
The parties celebrate their new alliance aboard Pompey’s ship. Soldiers and leaders drink heavily, and Lepidus becomes embarrassingly drunk. Menas proposes assassinating the triumvirs, but Pompey refuses such treachery. The revelry underscores the instability of their peace.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene I
In Syria, Ventidius reports his victory over the Parthians. He worries that further success may threaten Antony’s reputation, so he avoids pushing too far. Ventidius honors the fallen Pacorus. The scene highlights loyalty and caution within Antony’s ranks.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene II
Caesar, Lepidus, and Octavia speak warmly before she departs to join Antony. Caesar expresses love and concern for his sister. They exchange hopeful words about the peace her marriage may bring. The scene contrasts political duty with familial tenderness.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene III
Cleopatra sends her messenger to assess Octavia’s physical appearance. The messenger reports that Octavia is unimpressive in stature and looks. Cleopatra is relieved and regains confidence. This brief scene reinforces Cleopatra’s insecurity and competitiveness.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene IV
Octavia meets Antony, expressing hope that her marriage will unite the Roman leaders. Antony tries to assure her despite mounting political tensions. They discuss Caesar’s power and Antony’s alliances. Their marriage appears politically useful but emotionally distant.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene V
In Athens, Octavia learns that Caesar has raised armies against Antony. She is distressed that the political harmony she hoped to secure is already failing. A messenger reveals Caesar’s aggressive actions. Octavia prepares to mediate between the two men.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene VI
Caesar condemns Antony for returning to Cleopatra and giving her regions of his empire. He presents Antony as reckless and disloyal. Caesar’s advisors encourage him to act decisively. War between the two men becomes inevitable.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene VII
Antony prepares for battle but insists on fighting by sea, against the advice of his generals. Cleopatra offers to support him with her fleet. Enobarbus worries that Antony’s judgment is clouded by love. The scene foreshadows disaster.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene VIII
Caesar arranges his naval forces and prepares for the coming battle. He trusts in the discipline and strength of his officers. His strategic clarity contrasts with Antony’s emotional leadership. The conflict edges closer.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene IX
Antony stations himself on a hill to observe the sea battle. He anxiously watches the fighting from a distance. His generals express concern about Cleopatra’s involvement. The scene builds tension before the turning point.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene X
Cleopatra’s ships retreat during the battle, and Antony follows them, abandoning the fight. His forces collapse in disarray. Canidius declares he will now serve Caesar. Antony’s downfall accelerates dramatically.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene XI
Ashamed, Antony returns to Cleopatra. He blames her for his defeat, accusing her of betrayal. Cleopatra begs forgiveness, and Antony’s anger melts into despair and renewed affection. He resolves to continue resisting Caesar.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene XII
Caesar sends Thidias to deliver terms to Cleopatra, offering her mercy if she abandons Antony. Cleopatra gives a cautious, diplomatic answer. Antony discovers the meeting, publicly whipping Thidias and reasserting his dominance. The scene deepens Antony’s desperation.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act III-Scene XIII
Antony mourns his lost greatness but rallies himself for another fight. Cleopatra reassures him and pledges her loyalty. Enobarbus, however, secretly decides to defect to Caesar. Antony prepares for a final stand, clinging to hope.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene I
Caesar confidently arranges troop positions for the next battle. He dismisses Antony’s threats and focuses on a decisive victory. His officers prepare eagerly. Caesar appears fully in control.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene II
Antony bids a heartfelt farewell to his servants, sensing the end may be near. His emotional speech contrasts with his earlier pride. The soldiers weep, showing their deep loyalty. Antony is humbled by their devotion.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene III
Mysterious music is heard at night, interpreted as Hercules abandoning Antony. Soldiers consider it an omen of Antony’s failing fortune. They prepare for battle despite their unease. The supernatural tone underscores Antony’s decline.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene IV
Antony and Cleopatra arm themselves together. Cleopatra insists on participating in the battle, sharing Antony’s fate. Their intimate preparation blends romance and war. They depart with renewed resolve.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene V
Antony learns that Enobarbus has defected. He responds with unexpected generosity, sending Enobarbus’ treasure after him. Antony grieves his friend’s betrayal but refuses to condemn him. The moment highlights Antony’s tragic nobility.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene VI
Caesar welcomes Enobarbus, who is ashamed to receive Antony’s generous gift. Caesar prepares for the final confrontation. Enobarbus’ guilt deepens. His allegiance shift feels spiritually costly.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene VII
Antony achieves a brief military victory, lifting his spirits. He praises his soldiers and thanks Cleopatra for her support. Confidence returns, though temporarily. The triumph contrasts sharply with earlier losses.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene VIII
Antony celebrates with his troops and embraces Cleopatra joyfully. The couple seems restored and hopeful. Antony distributes praise and honors. The moment is triumphant but fragile.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene IX
Enobarbus, overwhelmed by guilt for betraying Antony, collapses. He delivers a final reflective speech on honor and loyalty. Alone and broken, he dies of despair. His death marks the end of Antony’s most loyal companion.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene X
Antony positions his forces and prepares for Caesar’s assault. He senses the tides turning again. The scene is brief but grim. Antony braces for defeat.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene XI
Caesar orders his forces into battle, confident of victory. He predicts that Antony’s troops will weaken. The preparations move swiftly. The final conflict is imminent.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene XII
Antony learns Cleopatra’s fleet has surrendered again, believing she betrayed him. Furious and devastated, he condemns her. Cleopatra flees to her monument for safety. Antony plunges deeper into despair.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene XIII
Cleopatra, fearing for her life, takes refuge in the monument and sends word to Antony that she has killed herself. She hopes to calm his rage and protect herself. Her deception has tragic consequences. She awaits news anxiously.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act IV-Scene XV
Antony is lifted to Cleopatra’s monument and dies in her arms. They share a final, emotional farewell. Cleopatra is devastated, realizing the extent of what has been lost. She resolves that life without Antony is meaningless.

Caesar learns of Antony’s death and reacts with solemn respect. He orders Cleopatra to be captured but treated with dignity. Proculeius is sent to secure her safely. Caesar reflects on Antony’s greatness and the tragic end of civil war.

Antony_and_Cleopatra-Act V-Scene II
Cleopatra refuses to be displayed in Caesar’s triumph and chooses to die on her own terms. She arranges for a poisonous asp to be smuggled to her. After dressing regally, she embraces death as a final act of autonomy. Her attendants follow her in suicide, ending the tragic story.

Orlando laments to Adam that his older brother Oliver mistreats him and denies him the education and respect due to a gentleman. Oliver arrives and mocks Orlando, provoking him into a confrontation. Their fight reveals deep resentment and long-standing abuse. After Orlando’s departure, Oliver secretly orders Charles the wrestler to harm Orlando in their upcoming wrestling match.

As_You_Like_It-Act I-Scene II
Rosalind and Celia discuss love and fortune as they watch the preparations for the wrestling match. Orlando arrives and, despite warnings, insists on competing. He surprises everyone by defeating Charles. Rosalind, moved by Orlando’s courage, gives him a necklace, and Orlando instantly falls in love. The Duke, however, reacts coldly after learning Orlando is the son of his banished enemy.

As_You_Like_It-Act I-Scene III
Duke Frederick grows suspicious of Rosalind and abruptly banishes her from court. Celia, refusing to abandon her cousin, decides to flee with her. They disguise themselves—Rosalind as the young man Ganymede and Celia as Aliena—and plan to seek Duke Senior in the Forest of Arden. Touchstone, the court fool, joins them. Their escape marks a shift from courtly intrigue to pastoral adventure.

As_You_Like_It-Act II-Scene I
Duke Senior and his loyal followers live a rustic but peaceful life in the Forest of Arden. They reflect on the natural world, finding philosophical meaning and freedom in exile. Although the outdoors can be harsh, they value its honesty compared to court life. Duke Senior expresses his readiness to welcome any who suffer injustice.

As_You_Like_It-Act II-Scene II
Duke Frederick discovers Rosalind, Celia, and Touchstone have vanished and suspects they followed Orlando. Furious, he commands that Orlando be sought and brought before him. He orders Oliver to find Orlando within a year or forfeit his property. The tension at court deepens while the main characters move farther into the forest world.

As_You_Like_It-Act II-Scene III
Adam warns Orlando that Oliver plans to burn him alive while he sleeps. He urges Orlando to flee and offers his life savings to support him. Touched by Adam’s loyalty, Orlando agrees to take him to the Forest of Arden. Their departure parallels the earlier escape of Rosalind and Celia.

As_You_Like_It-Act II-Scene IV
Rosalind (as Ganymede), Celia (as Aliena), and Touchstone arrive exhausted in the forest. They encounter the shepherds Corin and Silvius, who discuss Silvius’ hopeless love for the disdainful Phebe. Rosalind sympathizes with the pains of love but remains hidden in disguise. Celia proposes they purchase a cottage and land to settle safely in the forest.

As_You_Like_It-Act II-Scene V
Amiens sings a pastoral song as Jaques listens with pleasure. Jaques, amused by the melancholy beauty of forest life, requests more songs. Their interaction reveals Jaques’ blend of cynicism and romanticism. The scene emphasizes the play’s musical, idyllic tone.

As_You_Like_It-Act II-Scene VI
Orlando struggles to support Adam, who is weak with hunger and fatigue. Adam begs Orlando to leave him, believing he is too great a burden. Orlando refuses and vows to find food for them both. Their loyalty to each other highlights the play’s theme of genuine friendship.

As_You_Like_It-Act II-Scene VII
Duke Senior and his followers welcome Orlando and Adam after Orlando bursts in demanding food. Orlando apologizes for his rough entrance, and the Duke graciously offers hospitality. Jaques delivers his famous “All the world’s a stage” monologue, reflecting on the seven ages of man. The scene blends comedy, wisdom, and reconciliation.

As_You_Like_It-Act III-Scene I
Duke Frederick confronts Oliver and orders him to produce Orlando. When Oliver resists, Frederick seizes his property and banishes him unless he delivers Orlando within a year. Oliver is shaken by the Duke’s harshness. He begins to realize the consequences of his cruelty.

As_You_Like_It-Act III-Scene II
In the forest, Orlando hangs love poems for Rosalind on the trees. Rosalind (disguised as Ganymede) and Celia discover the poems and tease their excessive romanticism. Orlando appears, and Rosalind, pretending to be a love–cure expert, offers to “cure” him by letting him court her as if she were Rosalind. As_You_Like_It-Act III-Scene III
Touchstone courts the country girl Audrey with comical earnestness. The foolish priest, Sir Oliver Martext, arrives to marry them, but Jaques intervenes, suggesting they wait for a proper ceremony. Touchstone grudgingly agrees. The scene parodies romantic and pastoral conventions.

As_You_Like_It-Act III-Scene IV
Rosalind worries that Orlando has not come for their usual meeting. Celia tries to comfort her. Corin enters and invites them to watch Silvius declare love to Phebe. Phebe cruelly rejects Silvius, but after Rosalind (as Ganymede) reprimands her, Phebe suddenly falls in love with Ganymede.

As_You_Like_It-Act III-Scene V

As_You_Like_It-Act IV-Scene II
Jaques encounters several foresters carrying a deer they have hunted. They sing a humorous, ritualistic song about the death of the stag. Jaques reflects on the melancholy symbolism of the wounded animal. The scene maintains the forest’s contemplative mood.

As_You_Like_It-Act IV-Scene III
Oliver, having been attacked by a lioness, is saved by Orlando. Deeply moved, Oliver repents his past cruelty. He meets Rosalind and Celia and describes Orlando’s injuries. Rosalind faints at the news, betraying her true emotions despite her disguise.

As_You_Like_It-Act V-Scene I
Touchstone encounters William, a simple country youth who is also courting Audrey. Touchstone intimidates William with absurd threats and claims Audrey for himself. His mock rivalry pokes fun at romantic bravado. The scene adds comedic texture while advancing the Touchstone–Audrey subplot.

As_You_Like_It-Act V-Scene II

As_You_Like_It-Act V-Scene III
Touchstone and Audrey prepare for their wedding as two comic pages sing a lighthearted song about the passing of time and love’s constancy. Touchstone mocks them but enjoys the performance. The brief scene is a playful interlude before the story’s resolution.

As_You_Like_It-Act V-Scene IV
Rosalind reveals her true identity, transforming confusion into celebration. She unites the pairs: Rosalind with Orlando, Celia with Oliver, Phebe with Silvius, and Touchstone with Audrey. Duke Senior returns to claim his rightful place, and Duke Frederick’s sudden conversion ends the political conflict. The play concludes with music, joy, and a restored community.

Coriolanus-Act I-Scene I
A crowd of Roman citizens riot over the scarcity of grain, blaming Caius Martius for their suffering. Their anger is interrupted by Menenius, who pacifies them with a fable about the body and its members, reminding them of their dependence on patrician leadership. Martius arrives and harshly reprimands the citizens, calling them ungrateful and fickle. News comes that the Volscians, led by their champion Tullus Aufidius, threaten Rome. Martius eagerly prepares for battle.
